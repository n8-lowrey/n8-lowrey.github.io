ENGINEERING / CASE STUDY

Lunar Terrain Vehicle

Designing a conceptual lunar exploration vehicle while learning CAD and leading a ten-person team under a five-day deadline.

ORGANIZATION
NASA Moonshot Design Challenge
ROLE / STATUS
Lead Design Manager
Completed
FOCUS
Autodesk Inventor · Conceptual design · Leadership

01 / PROJECT OVERVIEW

Project overview

As part of the NASA Moonshot Design Challenge, I joined a ten-person team tasked with developing a conceptual lunar terrain vehicle for a larger simulated mission inspired by the Artemis program.

Twenty-four teams worked on different aspects of a proposed lunar mission focused on establishing a base for research, exploration, and communication. Our assignment was to research, design, and model a vehicle for extended exploration away from the base.

02 / DESIGN REQUIREMENTS

Design requirements

The proposed vehicle needed to support two astronauts during multiple days of independent operation on the lunar surface.

  • Withstand extreme lunar temperatures and challenging terrain.
  • Provide space for two astronauts to live and conduct research.
  • Collect, store or tow, and return samples to the lunar base.
  • Integrate these needs into a coherent conceptual design.

The entire effort operated under an intensive five-day deadline.

03 / MY CONTRIBUTION

My contribution

My teammates elected me Lead Design Manager by majority vote. I coordinated design research, delegated tasks, helped with research myself, and took responsibility for compiling our findings into the final CAD model.

I entered the challenge with no previous CAD experience. To meet the deadline, I taught myself Autodesk Inventor through online resources and extensive trial and error while continuing to coordinate the design work.

Outcome: I delivered the CAD model on time, and our team placed fourth out of 24 teams.

04 / REFLECTION: LEADERSHIP THROUGH FAILURE

Reflection: Leadership through failure

Although our team finished fourth out of 24 teams, I still consider this experience one of my greatest personal failures—and one of my most valuable learning experiences.

When I accepted the role of Lead Design Manager, I underestimated the difficulty of producing a complete CAD model with no prior experience. I was determined to learn the software and deliver a finished design, but the time I spent overcoming that learning curve came at a cost.

Despite completing the model before the deadline, I wasn’t able to incorporate all the research my teammates had worked so hard to compile. This created a disconnect between the design we presented and the research supporting it. I believe that disconnect may have prevented us from achieving a stronger placement.

What bothered me most wasn’t our ranking. It was knowing that my limitations had prevented me from fully representing my teammates’ contributions.

That experience changed the way I think about leadership. I learned that determination and hard work, while important, aren’t always enough. A good leader needs to recognize their limitations, trust the people around them, and know when to ask for help.

Most importantly, I learned that leading by example doesn’t mean having all the answers. Sometimes, it means being the first person willing to admit that you don’t.

I still take pride in what our team accomplished in just five days. But the lessons I took away from that experience have stayed with me far longer than our fourth-place finish.
